CAMPER 07

Published at HRI '262025

A robot that runs camp games on its own

I led development of a fully autonomous QTrobot system for seven UNBC Active Minds summer camps. Kids signed in by showing the ArUco marker on their lanyard, then played ten learning games with the robot while the researchers stayed out of the room. Everything ran offline: ROS for speech and motion, Django for the games, and a local language model for conversation. About 150 children aged 6 to 12 took part.

  • OpenCV

Under review2026

Screen-free games with two robots

For the follow-up study, our team swapped the touchscreen for things kids can pick up. We built five tangible games, hosted by a Misty II and a Reachy Mini. I led the team, built Tangram Quest from scratch, and wired up the Raspberry Pi game boards with RFID readers, capacitive touch and light-up buttons. A camera reads ArUco-tagged pieces and a local Llama model gives hints. The robots ran across two months of summer camps, and I coded hours of video with a custom behaviour codebook in BORIS.

Under review2026

How well do robots hear kids?

Most speech recognizers are built for adult voices. I ran a QTrobot fully offline in an elementary school over twelve session days and recorded 110 children aged 6 to 12 repeating words, repeating sentences and telling stories. Then I compared six offline speech recognizers, from Whisper variants to NVIDIA's Parakeet, against the robot's built-in decoder using word and character error rates.

In progress2026

Which small LLM should a classroom robot use?

My last thesis study. Educators send in curriculum questions with their own reference answers. Five open models small enough to run on a robot answer them, and the educators rate each anonymized answer for accuracy, clarity, age-appropriateness, teaching value and safety. The goal is a practical answer to which model belongs on a robot in front of children.

  • Ollama

What kids taught me about robots

Lessons from putting robots in front of a few hundred children.

Seven minutes goes fast.

Our 2025 camp sessions often ended right as kids were getting into a game. Longer turns and more robots are now part of the plan.

Little kids want a teammate.

83% of 6 to 8 year-olds felt they were playing with a partner. Only 45% of 9 to 12 year-olds said the same.

Noticing beats talking.

When a kid places the right piece and the robot misses it, nothing the robot says next can fix the moment. Reliable sensing comes first.

Consent is a project too.

Families have questions, and some prefer paper forms. Meeting parents early saves a last-minute scramble on day one.
